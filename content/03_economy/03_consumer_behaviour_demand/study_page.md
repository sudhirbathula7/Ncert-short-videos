# High-Yield Revision Sheet: Consumer Behaviour & Demand

# Consumer Behaviour & Demand
*Subject: Microeconomics | NCERT Class 12 Ch. 2 | UPSC & State PSC Core*

---

### ⚡ 30-Second Snapshot
- Consumer optimization resolves a fundamental trade-off: maximizing personal satisfaction while working within a fixed household budget.
- **Cardinal Utility** assumes satisfaction can be counted in numbers, whereas **Ordinal Utility** recognizes that consumers simply rank their preferences from best to worst.
- An Indifference Curve slopes downward and curves inward toward the origin because as you acquire more of one item, you are willing to give up less and less of the other to stay equally happy.
- Consumer equilibrium is reached at the exact point where the budget line just touches the highest reachable indifference curve—the point where personal preference matches market prices.
- On a straight-line demand curve, price sensitivity is not uniform: it is extremely high at expensive prices near the top, balances evenly at the exact center, and drops toward zero at cheap prices near the bottom.

### 📊 Utility Frameworks & Consumer Optimization Matrix

| Analytical Dimension | Cardinal Utility Analysis | Ordinal Utility Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| **Core Measurement Idea** | Absolute satisfaction measured in numerical units called utils. | Relative ranking of combinations from most preferred to least preferred. |
| **Pioneering Theorists** | Alfred Marshall. | J.R. Hicks and R.G.D. Allen. |
| **Governing Law** | Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility: each additional unit gives less satisfaction than the previous one. | Diminishing Marginal Rate of Substitution: willingness to trade away a good declines as you have less of it. |
| **Equilibrium Condition** | Money spent on each good brings the exact same extra satisfaction per rupee. | Tangency point: consumer willingness to substitute two goods matches the ratio of their market prices. |
| **Core Assumption** | The satisfaction derived from money itself stays constant as you spend. | Monotonic preferences: a consumer always prefers having more of a good rather than less. |

---

### 📐 Elasticity & Total Expenditure Mechanics

- **Price Sensitivity Along a Straight Demand Line:**
  - **Upper Half (Expensive Zone):** High price sensitivity. Because the price is already high, even a modest price cut triggers a large percentage jump in purchases.
  - **Exact Midpoint (Balanced Zone):** Unitary sensitivity. The percentage change in price matches the percentage change in quantity demanded.
  - **Lower Half (Cheap Zone):** Low price sensitivity. Because the item is already cheap, price changes barely move the needle on the percentage quantity purchased.
- **How Price Changes Affect Total Spending:**
  - **When Demand is Highly Sensitive:** Price and total expenditure move in opposite directions. Lowering prices increases total spending because sales volume jumps significantly.
  - **When Demand is Inelastic:** Price and total expenditure move in the exact same direction. A price drop lowers total spending because sales volume barely rises.
  - **When Demand is Unitary:** Total spending remains unchanged when the price fluctuates.

---

### ⚠️ Common UPSC Exam Traps
- **Trap 1 (Slope vs. Elasticity):** A straight-line demand curve has a steady, unchanging slope, but its price responsiveness is completely different at every level—high at the top, moderate in the middle, and low at the bottom.
- **Trap 2 (Movement vs. Shift):** A change in an item's own price only moves you along the existing demand curve. Shifts of the entire curve occur only when outside factors change, such as consumer income, tastes, or the prices of competing goods.
- **Trap 3 (Substitute vs. Complement Behavior):** For substitutes like tea and coffee, a price rise in one increases demand for the other. For complements like cars and fuel, a price rise in one drags down demand for the other.
- **Trap 4 (Indifference Curves Crossing):** Indifference curves can never cross each other. If they crossed, it would mean a single bundle of goods delivers two completely different levels of satisfaction at the same time.

### 🎯 High-Yield Facts Box
| Economic Concept | Intuitive Meaning | Strategic Policy Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Budget Constraint** | The absolute boundary of what a consumer can afford with their current income | Defines purchasing limits for welfare calculations |
| **Budget Line Slope** | The real-world trade-off between two goods set by store prices | Shows how market price ratios dictate consumer purchasing power |
| **Marginal Rate of Substitution** | The rate at which a consumer is personally willing to swap one good for another | Reveals true subjective valuation without needing artificial numbers |
| **Inferior Goods** | Items people buy less of as their income goes up | Guides targeting for subsidized public food grains and ration systems |
| **Inelastic Farm Demand** | People do not eat significantly more basic staples just because prices drop | Explains why bumper harvests can cause farm income to crash |

### 📝 Prelims Practice Challenge

**Q1. Consider the following statements regarding consumer choice and demand curves:**
1. Indifference curves bow inward toward the origin because consumers become less willing to surrender a good as it becomes scarce in their hands.
2. A straight-line demand curve maintains the exact same price elasticity at every single point along the line.
3. If two items are direct substitutes, an increase in the price of one will cause an outward shift in the demand for the other.

*Which of the statements given above are correct?*
- (A) 1 and 2 only
- (B) 2 and 3 only
- (C) 1 and 3 only
- (D) 1, 2, and 3

> **Answer:** **(C) 1 and 3 only**  
> **Explanation:** Statement 1 is correct because the marginal rate of substitution diminishes as a consumer acquires more of one good. Statement 2 is incorrect because while the physical slope of a straight line is constant, price sensitivity continuously drops from high to low as you travel downward. Statement 3 is correct because consumers shift to alternative products when the competing good becomes costlier.

**Q2. Consider the following pairs:**

| Economic Scenario | Observed Real-World Outcome |
|---|---|
| 1. **Unitary Elastic Demand** | Total spending by buyers remains steady even if market prices shift |
| 2. **Complementary Goods** | A price rise in one item raises buyer demand for the paired item |
| 3. **Midpoint of Linear Demand** | Total responsiveness is zero regardless of price movements |

*How many of the pairs given above are correctly matched?*
- (A) Only one pair
- (B) Only two pairs
- (C) All three pairs
- (D) None of the pairs

> **Answer:** **(A) Only one pair** *(Pair 1 is correct)*  
> **Explanation:** Pair 1 is correct: with unitary sensitivity, price and quantity changes offset each other, leaving total spending unchanged. Pair 2 is incorrect because complementary items move together: costlier fuel reduces the demand for automobiles. Pair 3 is incorrect because the midpoint represents balanced unitary responsiveness, while zero responsiveness occurs only at the very bottom where the good is essentially free.

### 🖋️ Mains Practice Question (10 Marks / 150 Words)

**Question:** *"Explain why basic food staples exhibit low price sensitivity. How does this characteristic create an economic paradox for farmer incomes during bumper harvest seasons?"*

#### 💡 Structured Answer Framework:
- **Introduction (25–30 words):**  
  Define price sensitivity as how strongly consumer purchases react to price changes. Explain that staple foods are biological necessities, meaning consumers do not drastically increase consumption when prices fall.
- **Body Arguments (80–90 words):**  
  - *The Consumption Ceiling:* Unlike luxury items, household food intake is capped by physical need. A sharp drop in wheat or pulse prices does not lead families to double their daily meals.
  - *The Revenue Trap:* Because consumer demand barely expands, an oversupply of crops causes farm-gate prices to collapse drastically in open markets. The steep price fall outweighs the extra volume sold, shrinking total farm earnings.
  - *The Paradox:* Individual success in raising productivity turns into collective financial distress across rural farming belts.
- **Conclusion / Way Forward (25–30 words):**  
  Resolving this structural dilemma requires institutional safeguards: active government price support operations, state procurement floors, and investment in storage and food-processing infrastructure to absorb seasonal surpluses.
