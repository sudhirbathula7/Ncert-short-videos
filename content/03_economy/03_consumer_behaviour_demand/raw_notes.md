## Core Factual & Conceptual Architecture

### 1. The Consumer's Optimization Problem
- **Objective**: Maximize total satisfaction while staying within a fixed, given budget limit.
- **Two goods abstraction**: Consuming specific quantities of Good One and Good Two to form a combined consumption bundle.
- **Dual analytical frameworks**:
  - **Cardinal Utility Theory**: Assumes consumer satisfaction can be measured numerically in units called utils using an additive utility function.
  - **Ordinal Utility Theory**: Assumes satisfaction cannot be measured numerically, but can be ranked by ordering commodity bundles based on consumer preferences (the Hicks-Allen framework).

### 2. Cardinal Utility Foundations
- **Total Utility**: The cumulative subjective satisfaction derived from consuming a total aggregate quantity of a good, calculated as the sum of marginal utilities of all units consumed.
- **Marginal Utility**: The extra satisfaction gained from consuming one additional unit of a good, calculated as the change in total utility divided by the change in the quantity consumed, or alternatively as the total utility of the current quantity minus the total utility of the previous quantity.
- **Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility (Gossen's First Law)**: As a consumer continues to consume more consecutive units of a homogeneous good without time gaps, the marginal utility gained from each additional unit steadily decreases.
- **Consumer Equilibrium (Single Good)**: Reached when the marginal utility of a good equals its price in monetary terms, or when the marginal utility per unit of money spent on that good equals the marginal utility of money.

### 3. Ordinal Utility & Indifference Curve Mechanics
- **Axiom of Monotonic Preferences**: A rational consumer always strictly prefers a consumption bundle that contains more of at least one good and no less of another, assuming non-satiation.
- **Indifference Curve**: A graphical curve representing all the different combinations of two goods that provide the consumer with the exact same level of total satisfaction.
- **Properties of Indifference Curves**:
  1. They slope downward from left to right, reflecting the trade-off where getting more of one good requires giving up some of the other.
  2. They are convex to the origin because of the Law of Diminishing Marginal Rate of Substitution.
  3. Higher indifference curves located further to the right represent strictly higher levels of satisfaction.
  4. Two indifference curves can never intersect or cross, as this would violate consistency and transitivity in consumer choices.
- **Marginal Rate of Substitution**: The slope of the indifference curve, which measures the rate at which a consumer is willing to substitute units of Good Two in exchange for an extra unit of Good One while keeping total satisfaction constant; it is equal to the ratio of their marginal utilities.

### 4. The Budget Constraint
- **Budget Set**: The collection of all possible consumption bundles that a consumer can afford to buy given their total nominal income and the market prices of the goods.
- **Budget Line Equation**: The line representing combinations where total expenditure on Good One plus total expenditure on Good Two exactly equals total nominal income.
- **Slope of the Budget Line**: The negative ratio of the price of Good One to the price of Good Two, which represents the market-determined trade-off ratio or opportunity cost between the two goods.
- **Shifts versus Rotations**:
  - When consumer income changes while prices remain constant, the budget line shifts outward (for an increase in income) or inward (for a decrease in income) in a parallel manner.
  - When the price of one good changes while income and other prices remain constant, the budget line pivots or rotates along the axis corresponding to the affected good.

### 5. Consumer Equilibrium (Ordinal Framework)
- **Equilibrium condition**: Achieved at the point where the budget line is strictly tangent to the highest possible indifference curve.
- **Tangency rule**: At the equilibrium point, the slope of the indifference curve equals the slope of the budget line, meaning the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio, or the marginal utility per price is equalized across both goods.
- **Interpretation**: At this point, the consumer's subjective willingness to trade goods perfectly matches the market's objective price ratio.

### 6. Demand Functions & Cross-Relationships
- **Law of Demand**: The inverse relationship stating that as the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, and vice versa, assuming all other factors remain constant.
- **Income Classification**:
  - **Normal Goods**: Goods for which demand increases when consumer income increases, holding other factors constant.
  - **Inferior Goods**: Goods for which demand decreases when consumer income increases, holding other factors constant.
- **Cross-Price Relationships**:
  - **Substitutes**: Goods where an increase in the price of one good leads to an increase in the demand for the other good, such as tea and coffee.
  - **Complements**: Goods where an increase in the price of one good leads to a decrease in the demand for the other good, such as cars and petrol, or tea and sugar.
- **Movement along versus Shift**:
  - **Change in Quantity Demanded**: A movement along a stationary demand curve caused purely by a change in the good's own price, while all other determinants of demand are held constant.
  - **Change in Demand**: A complete structural shift of the entire demand curve driven by changes in consumer income, preferences, or the prices of related goods.

### 7. Price Elasticity of Demand
- **Point Elasticity**: The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price at a specific point on the demand curve, calculated as the change in quantity divided by the change in price, multiplied by the ratio of price to quantity.
- **Linear Demand Curve Elasticity**:
  - At the exact midpoint of a straight-line demand curve, elasticity is equal to one, representing unitary elasticity.
  - In the upper half of the curve toward the price intercept, elasticity is greater than one (elastic demand), approaching infinity as it nears the vertical axis.
  - In the lower half of the curve toward the quantity intercept, elasticity is less than one (inelastic demand), approaching zero as it nears the horizontal axis.
- **Total Outlay or Expenditure Method**:
  - When demand is elastic (greater than one), the price of the good and total consumer expenditure move in opposite directions.
  - When demand is inelastic (less than one), the price and total consumer expenditure move in the same direction.
  - When demand has unitary elasticity (equal to one), total expenditure remains completely constant regardless of price movements.
