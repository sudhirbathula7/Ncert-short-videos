## Core Factual & Conceptual Architecture

### 1. Dynamics of Geomorphic Processes & Denudation
- Relief Imbalances: The terrestrial surface is shaped by two opposing forces: endogenic forces (internal energy, volcanism, and diastrophism causing crustal uplift/aggradation) and exogenic forces (solar-driven atmospheric forces causing surface degradation/gradation).
- Definitions:
  - Geomorphic Processes: Physical stresses and chemical actions exerted by endogenic and exogenic forces that alter the surface configuration of Earth materials.
  - Geomorphic Agent: A mobile natural medium (running water, groundwater, glaciers, wind, waves/currents) that acquires, transports, and deposits Earth material. Gravity is the underlying directional driver for downslope mass movements and fluid flow.
  - Diastrophism: Endogenic deformation encompassing orogeny (mountain building via intense folding in narrow crustal belts), epeirogeny (regional continental uplift or warping), earthquakes, and plate tectonics.
  - Denudation: The comprehensive umbrella term meaning "to strip off or uncover", integrating four continuous surface operations: weathering, mass wasting, erosion, and transportation.

### 2. Weathering Dynamics & Petrological Alteration
- Definition: The in-situ (on-site) mechanical disintegration and chemical decomposition of rocks by climatic elements, involving zero or negligible transportation of detached debris.
- Tripartite Taxonomy of Weathering:
  - Chemical Weathering: Decomposes rock minerals via chemical reactions requiring water, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and ambient heat. Processes include solution, carbonation, hydration, oxidation, and reduction.
  - Physical/Mechanical Weathering: Breaks rocks into smaller fragments without mineral alteration, driven by overburden pressure release, diurnal thermal expansion/contraction, frost wedging, and salt crystal growth.
  - Biological Weathering: Mineral breakdown and mechanical fracturing driven by living organisms (burrowing earthworms, termites, root wedging, microbial humic/carbonic acid exudation, and human agrarian cultivation).
- Exfoliation Mechanics: A structural result (not an independent primary process) where concentric shells or curved sheets flake off rock bodies due to expansion, contraction, and unloading, forming exfoliation domes and rounded corestone tors.
- Economic Significance: Prepares unconsolidated regolith for soil formation; facilitates mass movements; concentrates residual ore deposits (bauxite, iron, manganese) through the leaching away of soluble silica.

### 3. Mass Wasting & Slope Failure
- Definition: The direct downslope transfer of rock debris, regolith, and soil under the influence of gravity alone, without a mobile geomorphic transporting medium (water, wind, or ice) acting as the carrying vehicle.
- Triggering Mechanisms: Removal of base support (natural toe erosion or artificial road cuts), slope over-steepening, material surcharge, saturation from torrential precipitation, tectonic seismic tremors, and loss of binding root vegetation.
- Classification of Rapid Landslides:
  - Slump: Slippage of cohesive rock masses or debris accompanied by backward rotation along a curved slip plane.
  - Debris Slide: Rapid downslope sliding of unconsolidated regolith without backward rotation along a planar surface.
  - Debris Fall: Near-vertical free-fall of unconfined debris from overhanging vertical cliff faces.
  - Rockslide: Destructive, rapid planar sliding of stratified rock along structural joints, bedding planes, or fault fissures.
  - Rock Fall: Free gravitational drop of detached bedrock blocks from exposed cliff faces.
- Regional Landslide Vulnerability in India:
  - Himalayas: Characterized by frequent debris avalanches and massive rockslides due to active tectonic uplift, fragile unconsolidated sedimentary strata, steep relief, and intense monsoons.
  - Western Ghats & Nilgiris: Geologically stable, dense hard-rock shield terrains that still suffer catastrophic mudslides due to vertical fault scarps, deep mechanical weathering mantles, and localized high-intensity monsoonal precipitation.

### 4. Pedogenesis & Soil-Forming Determinants
- Soil Definition: A dynamic, unconsolidated natural body composed of weathered mineral fragments, decayed organic humus, living microorganisms, moisture, and soil air.
- The Five Soil-Forming Factors:
  - Parent Material (Passive): Controls initial soil texture, mechanical structure, and mineral/chemical composition.
  - Topography/Relief (Passive): Governs surface water runoff, soil depth, and solar exposure; steep slopes yield thin, immature soils, whereas flat uplands develop thick, mature horizons.
  - Climate (Active): The primary active factor; moisture controls chemical weathering, eluviation (downward leaching of soil constituents), and illuviation (subsurface deposition of leached minerals). High rainfall drives desilication (laterisation), arid regimes produce saline hardpans, and intermediate tropical regimes produce nodular calcium carbonate concretions (kankar).
  - Biological Activity (Active): Decomposes organic litter into humus; Rhizobium bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen in leguminous roots. Cold climates slow bacterial decay (accumulating peat in tundra), while humid tropical climates cause rapid oxidation and low humus retention.
  - Time (Passive): Determines profile maturity; young alluvial deposits lack distinct horizons, while ancient profiles exhibit well-developed A, B, and C master horizons.

### 5. Evolution of Fluvial Landforms (Running Water)
- Dynamic Drivers: Overland sheet flow transitions into linear stream flow, serving as the dominant geomorphic system in humid zones.
- Davisian Evolutionary Stages:
  - Youth: Shallow V-shaped valleys, poorly integrated streams, waterfalls, rapids, and broad flat drainage divides.
  - Maturity: Deep V-shaped valleys, integrated drainage, disappearance of waterfalls, expanding floodplains, and localized meanders.
  - Old Age: Low-gradient stream networks, extensive floodplains, cut-off oxbow lakes, natural levees, and ultimate reduction to a peneplain with isolated residual hills called monadnocks.
- Erosional Landforms:
  - Valleys: Rills evolve into gullies, which deepen and widen into V-shaped valleys, gorges (deep, steep-walled, uniform width in resistant rock), and canyons (stepped-scarp profiles in horizontal sedimentary strata).
  - Potholes & Plunge Pools: Circular depressions carved into rocky stream beds by circular grinding (abrasion) of rotating boulders; enlarged pools at the base of waterfalls form plunge pools.
  - Incised / Entrenched Meanders: Sinuous loop channels cut deep into hard bedrock due to base-level drops or tectonic crustal rejuvenation.
  - River Terraces: Step-like remnants of abandoned floodplains flanking a river channel; paired terraces mark balanced incision on both banks.
- Depositional Landforms:
  - Alluvial Fans: Cone-shaped sedimentary fans deposited where high-gradient mountain torrents abruptly enter low-gradient piedmont plains.
  - Deltas: Stratified triangular sediment deposits formed where a sediment-laden river enters standing marine or lacustrine water.
  - Floodplains & Natural Levees: Active floodplains (inundated river channels) and inactive floodplains flanked by natural levees (parallel ridges of coarse alluvium on channel margins).
  - Point Bars & Oxbow Lakes: Sedimentary accretion occurs along the convex (inner) slip-off bank, while active undercutting occurs along the concave (outer) cut bank. Extreme meander neck cut-offs isolate abandoned water loops called oxbow lakes.

### 6. Karst Landforms (Groundwater Dynamics)
- Prerequisites: Thick, dense, jointed limestones or dolomites (calcium carbonate) situated in regions with abundant groundwater circulation.
- Erosional Landforms:
  - Sinkholes: Funnel-shaped surface depressions formed by pure carbonate dissolution (solution sink) or subterranean cave-roof collapse (collapse sink/doline).
  - Uvalas: Extensive, irregular trench depressions formed by the coalescence of several adjacent sinkholes.
  - Lapies & Limestone Pavements: Intricate solution trenches, grooves, and sharp residual limestone ridges formed along rock joint sets.
  - Caves & Tunnels: Horizontal subterranean cavities carved along bedding planes; conduits open at both ends form natural tunnels.
- Depositional Landforms:
  - Stalactites: Icicle-like calcite columns hanging downward from cave ceilings, formed by the precipitation of calcium carbonate from evaporating groundwater drops.
  - Stalagmites: Broad, blunt calcite cones rising upward from cave floors directly beneath dripping stalactites.
  - Cave Pillars: Vertical columns formed by the complete fusion of an elongating stalactite with a rising stalagmite.

### 7. Glacial Landforms (Moving Ice)
- Dynamics: Masses of terrestrial ice moving downslope under gravitational deformation, executing profound erosion via plucking and basal abrasion.
- Erosional Landforms:
  - Cirque: Steep-sided, armchair-like or amphitheatre-shaped bedrock hollow situated at the head of a mountain glacial valley, often retaining a post-glacial tarn lake.
  - Horn & Arête: Sharp, pyramidal summits (horns like the Matterhorn or Everest) formed when three or more cirques erode headward toward a central peak; jagged, saw-toothed knife-edge dividing ridges are arêtes.
  - Glacial Trough (U-shaped Valley): Broad-floored, steep-sided valleys carved from pre-existing V-shaped valleys, featuring hanging valleys with waterfalls and submerged marine fjords.
- Depositional Landforms:
  - Glacial Till: Completely unsorted, unstratified, angular rock debris deposited directly by melting glacier ice.
  - Moraines: Ridges of unstratified till, classified as terminal/end moraines (at the ice terminus), lateral moraines (along valley sides), medial moraines (formed by converging lateral moraines), and ground moraines (blanketing the valley floor).
  - Outwash Plains: Broad plains of sorted, stratified sands and gravels deposited by braided glacio-fluvial meltwater streams.
  - Eskers: Sinuous, winding ridges of sorted gravel and sand deposited by sub-glacial meltwater tunnels.
  - Drumlins: Inverted spoon-shaped, smooth, streamlined hills of till aligned parallel to glacier flow; a steep stoss face points up-glacier, while a gentle tapering tail points down-glacier.

### 8. Coastal Landforms (Waves & Currents)
- Coastal Typology:
  - High Rocky Submerged Coasts: Erosional features dominate, characterized by wave-attack cliffs, offshore stacks, and deep fjords (e.g., India's west coast).
  - Low Sedimentary Emerged Coasts: Depositional features dominate, characterized by coastal sand dunes, expansive barrier beaches, lagoons, and river deltas (e.g., India's east coast).
- Erosional Landforms: Sea cliffs, wave-cut notches, wave-cut benches/platforms, sea caves, sea arches, and isolated sea stacks.
- Depositional Landforms: Sandy beaches, shingle beaches (coarse gravels), coastal sand dunes, offshore bars, barrier bars, spits (sediment ridges attached at one end to a headland), and enclosed coastal lagoons.

### 9. Aeolian & Arid Landforms (Wind & Sheetwash)
- Arid Dynamics: Wind functions via deflation (lifting loose particles), abrasion (sand-blasting up to 1-2 meters above ground), and saltation, while occasional flash floods carve relief.
- Erosional Landforms:
  - Pediments & Pediplains: Gently sloping rock-cut surfaces at the foot of desert mountains formed by lateral stream planation and sheetwash; parallel slope retreat (backwasting) reduces mountains to isolated, rounded bedrock hills called inselbergs, creating a flat pediplain.
  - Playas: Shallow, ephemeral desert drainage basins that form temporary salt lakes, leaving encrusted alkali flats upon evaporation.
  - Deflation Hollows, Pedestal & Mushroom Rocks: Depressions excavated by wind deflation, and rock pillars carved into mushroom shapes because wind abrasion is concentrated near ground level.
- Depositional Sand Dunes:
  - Barchans: Crescent-shaped dunes with convex backs and horns pointing downwind, formed under constant wind direction over bare, hard surfaces.
  - Parabolic Dunes: Reversed crescent-shaped dunes with horns pointing upwind, forming where sandy surfaces are partially stabilized by vegetative cover.
  - Seif: Longitudinal dunes with a single elongated wing, formed when shifting wind vectors alter a barchan's morphology.
  - Transverse Dunes: Linear sand ridges aligned perpendicular to the dominant wind direction, formed under abundant sand supplies.
  - Longitudinal Dunes: Long, narrow sand ridges aligned parallel to the prevailing wind direction under conditions of limited sand supply.
