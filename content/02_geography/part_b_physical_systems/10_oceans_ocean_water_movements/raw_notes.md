## Part 1: The Hydrological (Water) Cycle
Earth is called the "Blue Planet" due to its abundant surface water. The hydrological cycle is the continuous circulation of water between oceans, atmosphere, land surface, subsurface, and organisms.

- ~91% of planetary water is in the oceans; the rest is freshwater (glaciers/icecaps, groundwater, lakes, soil moisture, atmosphere, streams, organisms).
- ~59% of precipitation on land returns to atmosphere via evaporation; remainder runs off, infiltrates ground, or forms glaciers.
- Renewable water is roughly constant, but rising demand and pollution create regional crises.
- Key processes: Evaporation, evapotranspiration, sublimation, condensation, precipitation, runoff, infiltration, groundwater discharge.

## Part 2: Relief of the Ocean Floor
Oceans (Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, Arctic) merge without sharp boundaries. Most ocean floor lies 3–6 km below sea level, featuring rugged tectonic and depositional relief.

Four Major Divisions:
- Continental Shelf: Shallow, gently sloping extended margin (gradient <= 1°); ends at shelf break. Average width ~80 km (nearly absent off Chile; widest is Siberian Shelf in Arctic Ocean at ~1,500 km). Depth 30–600 m. Covered with sediments; prime source of fossil fuels.
- Continental Slope: Connects shelf to ocean basin; begins at sharp drop; gradient 2°–5°; depth 200–3,000 m; marks true continental edge; trenches and canyons occur here.
- Deep Sea Plain: Gently sloping, flattest part of basin; depth 3,000–6,000 m; covered in fine clay and silt.
- Oceanic Deeps (Trenches): Deepest narrow zones; 3–5 km deeper than surrounding floor; associated with active volcanoes and earthquakes. 57 known deeps (32 Pacific, 19 Atlantic, 6 Indian).

Minor Relief Features:
- Mid-oceanic Ridges: Interconnected mountain chains with central rift valleys (e.g., Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland).
- Seamount: Pointed underwater volcanic peak not reaching surface (e.g., Emperor Seamount).
- Submarine Canyons: Deep valleys cutting shelves/slopes (e.g., Hudson Canyon).
- Guyot: Flat-topped submerged seamount formed via gradual subsidence.
- Atoll: Low tropical coral ring surrounding a central lagoon.

## Part 3: Temperature of Ocean Waters
Factors affecting temperature:
- Latitude: Decreases from equator (~27°C) to poles (~0°C).
- Land-Water Distribution: Northern hemisphere oceans warmer (~19°C) than southern hemisphere (~16°C) due to contact with land.
- Prevailing Winds: Offshore winds cause upwelling of cold water; onshore winds pile up warm water.
- Ocean Currents: Warm currents raise temperatures (Gulf Stream); cold currents lower them (Labrador).

Vertical Temperature Structure (3 Layers):
- Top Warm Layer: ~500 m thick, 20°–25°C.
- Thermocline: 500–1,000 m thick zone of rapid temperature drop.
- Deep Cold Layer: Extends to floor; near 0°C; contains ~90% of total ocean volume.
- Polar regions lack thermocline: Surface water is already near 0°C, showing uniform vertical cold temperature.

## Part 4: Salinity of Ocean Waters
Salinity is dissolved salt content in parts per thousand (ppt or ‰). Normal open ocean is 33–37‰. Water below 24.7‰ is considered brackish.

Factors:
- Evaporation and precipitation.
- Freshwater river influx and ice melt/freeze.
- Wind and ocean currents.

Highest Salinity Water Bodies:
- Lake Van (Turkey): 330‰
- Dead Sea: 238‰
- Great Salt Lake (USA): 220‰
- Red Sea: up to 41‰ (high evaporation, enclosed).
- Regional Contrast: Arabian Sea has higher salinity than Bay of Bengal due to high evaporation and low freshwater river influx.
- Halocline: Zone of sharp salinity increase with depth. Dense saline water sinks, creating density stratification.

## Part 5: Movements of Ocean Water
- Horizontal: Currents (water mass translation) and Waves (wind energy moving through water molecules rotating in small orbits).
- Vertical: Tides (gravitational rise/fall), Upwelling (cold water rising), and Sinking (dense water descending).

Waves:
- Powered by wind. Wavelength, crest, trough, amplitude, wave period, wave speed (knots).
- Wave breaks when water depth is less than half the wavelength.

Tides:
- Cause: Gravitational pull of moon (dominant) and sun, counterbalanced by centrifugal force of rotating Earth.
- Bay of Fundy (Canada): World's highest tides (15–16 m).
- Types by Frequency: Semi-diurnal (2 high, 2 low daily), Diurnal (1 high, 1 low), Mixed.
- Types by Alignment:
  - Spring Tides: Sun, Moon, and Earth align in a straight line (Syzygy) at New Moon and Full Moon → Higher tidal range.
  - Neap Tides: Sun and Moon at right angles (Quadrature) → Forces counteract → Lower tidal range.
- Perigee (Moon closest) / Perihelion (Sun closest): Maximum tidal range. Apogee / Aphelion: Minimum tidal range.
- Importance: Navigation into shallow ports, desilting estuaries, tidal energy generation (e.g., Durgaduani, Sunderbans).

Ocean Currents:
- Drivers: Solar heating (thermal gradient expansion ~8 cm higher at equator), wind friction, gravity, Coriolis force (creates circular Gyres).
- Density variation: Cold/saline water sinks; warm/less saline water rises and flows poleward.
- Classification:
  - Surface (upper 400 m, 10% volume) vs Deep Water (90% volume, thermohaline driven).
  - Warm Currents (flow from low to high latitudes on eastern coasts in tropics, western coasts in mid/high latitudes).
  - Cold Currents (flow from high to low latitudes on western coasts in tropics, eastern coasts in high latitudes).
- Climatic Effects:
  - Cold currents cause coastal aridity/deserts (e.g., Atacama, Namib, Benguela).
  - Mixing zones of warm and cold currents replenish oxygen and nutrients, spurring plankton growth to form the world's richest fishing grounds (e.g., Gulf Stream meets Labrador Current off Newfoundland).
